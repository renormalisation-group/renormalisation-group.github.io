Dear Moor,

This morning in bed the following dialectical points about the natural sciences came into my head:

The object of science: matter in motion, bodies. Bodies cannot be separated from motion, their forms and kinds can only be apprehended in motion; nothing can be said about bodies divorced from motion, divorced from all relation to other bodies. Only in motion does the body reveal what it is. Hence natural science obtains knowledge about bodies by examining them in their relationship to each other, in motion. Cognition of the various forms of motion is cognition of bodies. The investigation of these various forms of motion is therefore the chief object of the natural sciences.1

Thus opens a letter by Friedrich Engels outlining what he would, over the next decade, explore in his Dialectics of Nature, a mass of essays, notes, and fragments that remained unpublished during his lifetime. The marriage of Hegelian philosophy and natural science that Engels officiated would over time develop into a novel, sophisticated, and contentious tradition in the philosophy of science that forms the subject of this series of articles.

It is useful to get a sense of the scientific milieu that formed the backdrop against which Engels authored his Dialectics of Nature. The nineteenth century was witness to a sea change in practically every branch of the natural sciences.

In chemistry, the French aristocrat Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier had opened up the field of quantitative chemistry, which Justus von Liebig then applied to the realm of organic compounds. In biology, the German botanist Matthias Schleiden had made a series of advances in cellular theory that his friend the physiologist Theodor Schwann extended to the animal world. In physics, William Robert Grove was carrying out pioneering work with the first fuel cell and anticipating the theory of the conservation of energy, while James Clerk Maxwell was taking Michael Faraday’s work on electricity toward a unified theory of electromagnetism.2

In Manchester, where Engels would live and work between 1849–1870, John Dalton was laying the foundations for the atomic classification of elements into the periodic table and his student James Joule would go on to establish the principle of conservation of energy.

Through a series of painstaking experiments, using his family’s beer-making technology, he demonstrated that the total amount of energy remains constant while being transferred from one source to another, in his case from the mechanical act of stirring into heat. William Thomson (the future Lord Kelvin) and the German scientists Hermann von Helmholtz and Rudolf Clausius used Joule’s results to formulate the first two laws of thermodynamics, establishing principles of the conservation of energy and of the universe’s ever-increasing entropy.3

It is interesting to note that many of the scientific advances of the age were not made at universities, but rather at industrial manufacturing centers: Leeds, Glasgow, Birmingham, and Manchester.

The effect of [the invention of the steam engine] and other useful applications of science, such as Franklin’s lightning conductor in 1752, made practical men see not only that in science they had a vast power which could be used profitably, but that in order to use it, it was necessary to go deeply into the secrets of nature. Towards the end of the [seventeenth] century a lively scientific interest began to penetrate these manufacturing circles, and it was there that most of the new developments of science took place… The effective center of scientific thought in England at the end of the eighteenth century was not the Royal Society but the Lunar Society which met at Birmingham under the patronage of Boulton, Wilkinson and Wedgwood, attended by Watt, Priestly, and Erasmus Darwin… It was useless to expect anything from universities; they had in the eighteenth century sunk to a depth of sloth, ignorance and bigotry worse than any in their history.4

Where in England scientific activity was bankrolled by industrialists, on the Continent, science would form a strong association with the state.

The French artillery schools were indeed the first formal institutions at which scientific education could be acquired. The great French mathematicians and physicists at the end of the period, like Lagrange, Laplace and Monge, etc., were trained at these schools… Lavoisier was at the same time a member of the financial oligarchy of the Fermiers Généraux and the scientific head of the Government Arsenal, in the laboratory of which most of his important experiments were made. In founding the École Polytechnique and the Bureau de Poids et Mesures, it created the first establishment of science on a fully subsidised state basis.5

In summation, nothing short of a tectonic shift in the scientific world.

Engels embodied this zeitgeist, this “enthusiasm of reason” wholly.6 In his preface to the Dialectics of Nature, the British-Indian scientist J. B. S. Haldane wrote:

[Engels] was probably the most widely educated man of his day. Not only had he a profound knowledge of economics and history, but he knew enough to discuss the meaning of an obscure Latin phrase concerning Roman marriage law, or the processes taking place when a piece of impure zinc was dipped into sulphuric acid.7

He kept company with a number of scientists (the chemists Carl Schorlemmer and Philipp Pauli, and the geologist John Roche Dakyns) and read extensively on advances in the natural sciences and mathematics throughout his life.

As early as 1858, Engels asked Marx to send him Hegel’s Philosophy of Nature, where he hoped to find premonitory statements that foresaw developments in the modern sciences.8 “I am exceedingly curious to see whether the old man [Hegel] may not already have had some inkling of them,” he wonders of the discovery of cells by Schleiden (in plants) and Schwann (in animals), writing that:

The cell is Hegelian ‘being in itself’ and its development follows the Hegelian process step by step right up to the final emergence of the ‘idea’ — i.e. each completed organism.

In the same letter, he describes Joule’s experiments on the interconvertibility of different forms of energy as “another result that would have delighted old Hegel,” asking: “Isn’t this splendid material proof of how the reflex categories dissolve one into the other?” This is followed by a observation on the similarities between humans and other species:

This much is certain — comparative physiology gives one a healthy contempt for man’s idealistic arrogance in regard to other animals. At every step it is forcibly brought home to one how completely his structure corresponds to that of other mammals; he has basic features in common with all vertebrates and even — if less distinctly — with insects, crustaceans, tapeworms, etc. Here too Hegel’s stuff about the qualitative leap in the quantitative sequence fits in very nicely.

John Bellamy Foster has noted that only thirteen days prior to the writing of this letter, the first announcement of the theory of evolution was made at the Linnaean Society by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russell Wallace.9 A year and a half later, soon after the publication of Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species, he wrote

Darwin, by the way, whom I’m reading just now, is absolutely splendid. There was one aspect of teleology that had yet to be demolished, and that has now been done. Never before has so grandiose an attempt been made to demonstrate historical evolution in Nature, and certainly never to such good effect.10

It is natural to wonder why Engels felt it was necessary to understand the recent developments in the natural sciences through the lens of the Hegelian dialectic at all. Surely these scientific advancements didn’t require any familiarity with Hegel’s work — what possible relevance could it have, then? A first pass at an answer, one that we shall content ourselves with until we have learned more, is supplied by Haldane:

He needed this knowledge because dialectical materialism, the philosophy which, along with Marx, he founded, is not merely a philosophy of history, but a philosophy which illuminates all events whatever, from the falling of a stone to a poet’s imaginings. And it lays particular emphasis on the inter-connection of all processes, and the artificial character of the distinctions which men have drawn, not merely between vertebrates and invertebrates or liquids and gases, but between the different fields of human knowledge such as economics, history, and natural science.11

The Dialectics of Nature was eventually published in 1927, when Engels’ literary executor Eduard Bernstein showed Albert Einstein drafts of the essays. Einstein regarded much of the science in it to be confused, but did not oppose its publication, believing it to be of historical interest.12 As we will see, it would be much, much more.

However, before we discuss the Dialectics of Nature, we will turn our attention to another, earlier text by Engels, where his general philosophical orientation and more specific perspectives on the natural sciences is clarified in a more complete fashion: Herr Eugen Dühring’s Revolution in Science, popularly known as Anti-Dühring.
